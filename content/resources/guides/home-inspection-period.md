The inspection window is where good deals get clearer. Your job is not to find a perfect house. Your job is to understand risk, cost, and whether you still want *this* house at *this* price.

## What a general inspection typically covers

At a high level, inspectors report on visible and accessible conditions related to:

- Structure and foundation indicators they can observe
- Roof (from accessible vantage points)
- HVAC, plumbing, and electrical systems
- Water intrusion clues
- Safety items (rails, detectors, GFCI concerns, etc.)

Inspectors do not rewrite the future. They document what they can see that day.

## Optional inspections worth considering

Depending on age, location, and what you see on the tour:

- Sewer scope (especially older lines or big trees)
- Roof specialty evaluation
- Pool/spa inspection
- Pest inspection
- Fireplace / chimney evaluation when relevant

Ask which add-ons make sense for the specific property — not for every property on earth.

## How to prioritize findings

1. **Safety and habitability**  
2. **Expensive systems near failure**  
3. **Active water issues**  
4. **Cosmetic and maintenance items**

A long report full of maintenance notes is normal for older homes. Translate the list into a plan.

## Negotiation posture

Match requests to the home’s age and price. Use the framework in [Oklahoma option period and inspection negotiations](/resources/guides/oklahoma-option-period-explained).

Tools may include repairs, credits, price changes, or walking away. Credits must fit lender rules.

## Sellers: reduce surprises

If you are listing, complete obvious fixes and clean thoroughly before photos. The [seller prep checklist](/resources/checklists/seller-prep-checklist) and [listing photo-ready checklist](/resources/checklists/listing-photo-ready-checklist) help you catch what buyers will notice first.

## Keep communication clean

- Put requests in writing
- Use deadlines
- Decide — don’t drag
- Separate “must fix” from “nice to fix”
